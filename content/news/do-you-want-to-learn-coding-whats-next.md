## Write down why {#why data-kicker="01 — Motivation"}

Is it curiosity? Are changes in your current field pushing you toward a new career? Do you want to build a product or a website but cannot yet hire a developer? Your reason matters, because motivation helps you continue when the work becomes difficult.

</div>

<ol class="news-phases news-phases--three news-wide">
  <li><strong>“I am curious whether I can learn.”</strong><p>Take time to explore the field and find a stronger reason before committing to a long program.</p></li>
  <li><strong>“I want to build a website or product.”</strong><p>A real project gives you somewhere to apply each new idea, and makes the learning easier to retain.</p></li>
  <li><strong>“I want to switch careers.”</strong><p>Research the work, speak with developers, try introductory tutorials, and understand the commitment before you begin.</p></li>
</ol>

<div class="news-head news-head--top news-wide">

## Make time {#time data-kicker="02 — Consistency"}

<div>

Consider whether you can set aside regular learning time. Thirty focused minutes each day, for at least six months, is more useful than an occasional long session.

<p class="news-pull">Consistency matters more than a dramatic start.</p>

</div>

</div>

## Learn the history and context {#context data-kicker="03 — Context"}

<div>

Understanding computers, their history, their effect on society, and the relationship between software and hardware makes programming concepts easier to place.

Read, watch introductory material, speak with a developer, or visit a software team. Context turns isolated syntax into a broader understanding of how systems work.

</div>

</div>

<div class="news-head news-wide">

## Plot your next move {#next data-kicker="04 — Getting started"}

Four ways to turn the decision into practice.

</div>

<ol class="news-phases news-wide">
  <li><strong>Have a project in mind</strong><p>Build something that solves a real problem for you, a community, a nonprofit, or a small organization. Applying a concept makes it concrete.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Use online resources</strong><p><a href="https://edx.org">edX</a>, <a href="https://codecademy.com">Codecademy</a>, <a href="https://freecodecamp.org">freeCodeCamp</a>, and <a href="https://coursera.org">Coursera</a> offer structured starting points; <a href="https://stackoverflow.com">Stack Overflow</a> can help when you get stuck.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Find a partner or community</strong><p>Pair programming and learning groups make difficult problems less isolating. Look for events and communities where you can ask questions.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Read widely</strong><p>Follow developers’ writing, watch talks, and read books. Learning how experienced people approach problems is as valuable as memorizing syntax.</p></li>
</ol>
